# **Bibliographic Integrity and Methodological Convergence in Academic Software Development: An Evaluation of the LabTech Experience Report**

The contemporary landscape of Information Technology (IT) in Brazil is characterized by a profound discrepancy between the rapid expansion of industrial demand and the traditional pedagogical structures of higher education. As digital transformation permeates every sector of the national economy, the necessity for educational models that bridge the gap between theoretical abstraction and professional realization has never been more urgent. The LabTech initiative at the Centro Universitário do Distrito Federal (UDF) represents a strategic response to this challenge, functioning as an Academic Software Factory (ASF) that integrates university extension with professional skill development. The following report provides a comprehensive analysis of the references, psychometric instruments, and methodological frameworks utilized in the LabTech experience report, verifying their existence, validity, and the adequacy of their application within the context of 21st-century computing education.

## **The Macroeconomic Imperative for Applied Computing Education**

The foundational justification for initiatives like LabTech is rooted in the explosive growth of the Brazilian technology sector. According to data produced by the Federação do Comércio de Bens, Serviços e Turismo do Estado de São Paulo (FecomercioSP) and disseminated by the Empresa Brasil de Comunicação, the number of employment positions in technology-related professions increased by 95% between 2012 and 2022\.1 This trajectory is not merely a quantitative increase in job openings but represents a qualitative shift in the Brazilian labor market's structure. The total number of active roles across 30 analyzed technology occupations rose from approximately 445,000 in 2012 to 868,100 in 2022\.3  
This growth is particularly pronounced in high-complexity roles. For instance, the position of operating systems engineer in computing witnessed an extraordinary elevation of 741.2% in formal employment links during this decade.1 Such data suggests an underlying trend toward the professionalization of the digital infrastructure, requiring a workforce capable of managing increasingly sophisticated systems. Other areas of significant expansion include computer science research, which grew by 579.3%, and IT management technology, which saw an increase of 450.7%.1

The regional context of Brasília is equally significant. As the nation's capital and a burgeoning tech hub, the Distrito Federal hosts a unique ecosystem of public institutions, private enterprises, and research centers. In 2020, Brasília maintained its position as the eighth-largest economy among Brazilian states, contributing approximately 3.5% of the national Gross Domestic Product (GDP).5 More importantly, the services sector, which encompasses the majority of IT activities, accounts for 95% of the local economy.7 This service-heavy economic structure provides a stable foundation for technology students, as the demand for digital solutions in both the public and private spheres remains high despite broader economic fluctuations.5 The article’s claim regarding Brasília’s status as a secondary hub for IT GDP is corroborated by the city’s leadership in per capita GDP, which is 2.2 times the national average, reflecting a high concentration of specialized labor and administrative headquarters.6

## **Theoretical Foundations of the Academic Software Factory**

The LabTech project is anchored in the "learning by doing" pedagogical philosophy, which prioritizes experiential learning as the primary mechanism for knowledge acquisition. The article correctly cites Bernardi (2017) as a cornerstone for this approach. The research conducted by Tainara Bernardi at the University of Passo Fundo emphasizes that Software Factories provide an ideal environment for professional training by applying theoretical knowledge to specific professional actions.10 This model facilitates the integration of students into real-world work processes, allowing them to encounter authentic problems that require both technical ingenuity and project management skills.12  
The evolution of the ASF model also draws from broader educational theories, including John Dewey’s conceptualization of democracy and education, which argues that learning is a social process and that schools should reflect the complexities of the society they serve.12 In the context of IT, this means moving beyond isolated coding exercises toward collaborative environments where the "hard skills" of programming are inextricably linked with the "soft skills" of teamwork, communication, and leadership.  
The role of university extension is central to this mission. Santos (2021) describes extension as a bridge between the university and the system of health or society, a concept that LabTech applies to the IT ecosystem.14 By developing software solutions for the local community—such as room reservation systems and event management platforms—LabTech students engage in "socialization of research products," ensuring that academic output has a direct social benefit.15 This extensionist approach also serves as a resilient educational strategy during times of crisis, such as the COVID-19 pandemic, where remote extension projects provided critical visibility to student skills and fostered digital inclusion.16

## **Psychometric Assessment of Entrepreneurial Competencies**

One of the most innovative aspects of the LabTech report is its use of the Entrepreneurship Competence Framework (EntreComp) to measure the development of interpersonal and organizational skills. Developed by the European Commission (Bacigalupo et al., 2016), EntreComp provides a comprehensive and shared definition of entrepreneurship as a transversal competence.18 The framework is designed to bridge the gap between the world of education and the world of work by defining 15 specific competencies across three interrelated areas: Ideas and Opportunities, Resources, and Into Action.20

| EntreComp Area | Building Block Competencies | Educational Implication |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Ideas & Opportunities** | Spotting opportunities, Creativity, Vision, Valuing ideas, Ethical/Sustainable thinking | Fosters innovative conceptualization and project feasibility analysis. |
| **Resources** | Self-awareness/Self-efficacy, Motivation/Perseverance, Mobilizing resources, Financial literacy, Mobilizing others | Focuses on the internal and external assets required to sustain a project. |
| **Into Action** | Taking initiative, Planning/Management, Coping with uncertainty/risk, Working with others, Learning through experience | Bridges the gap between conceptualization and professional realization. |

The LabTech report utilizes a 17-item questionnaire derived from this framework to evaluate student perceptions of their own abilities. Research into the psychometric properties of such self-assessment tools indicates that they are effective for capturing the multi-dimensional nature of entrepreneurial mindsets.22 For instance, factor analysis of EntreComp-based instruments often confirms four primary dimensions: Ideas and Opportunities, Personal Resources, Specific Knowledge, and Into Action.22 The high scores observed in the LabTech study—particularly in the dimension of "collaborative value creation" ($3.79/4.0$)—suggest that the ASF environment is highly effective at fostering the "Into Action" area of the framework.15 This area is critical for IT professionals, as it encompasses the ability to manage complex projects under conditions of uncertainty and risk.20

## **The Construct of General Self-Efficacy in Professional Maturity**

The inclusion of the General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSE) provides a psychological depth to the evaluation of LabTech. Originally proposed by Sherer et al. in 1982, self-efficacy refers to an individual's global confidence in their ability to perform the behaviors required to produce desired outcomes across a wide range of demanding situations.25 This construct is particularly relevant in the field of computing, where professionals must constantly adapt to new technologies and troubleshoot unforeseen technical failures.  
The GSE, as developed by Sherer, was constructed to measure a stable personality trait that reflects competence across various life domains, distinct from domain-specific efficacy.25 The original scale consists of 23 items, with 17 loading onto the factor of general self-efficacy and 6 loading onto social self-efficacy.26 The LabTech report utilizes a version with 20 items, which aligns with common adaptations of the scale used in international research, such as those that focus on willingness to initiate behavior, willingness to expend effort, and persistence in the face of adversity.26  
The scoring of the GSE requires careful attention to reverse-coded items to ensure the validity of the final escore. In the LabTech implementation, items 8, 11, 13, 16, 18, and 20 are recoded to ensure that a higher total score consistently represents higher perceived self-efficacy.15 This methodology is supported by the scale's high internal consistency, with Cronbach's alpha typically ranging between $0.75$ and $0.91$ across diverse populations.27  
The distribution of GSE scores in the LabTech sample (Mean \= $79.5$; Range $67$--$90$) suggests that participants possess a robust sense of personal mastery.15 This is a significant finding, as self-efficacy is a powerful predictor of career resilience, job-related performance, and the ability to cope with the high-stress environments characteristic of the IT industry.25 The integration of this metric allows the LabTech researchers to correlate psychological readiness with technical activity, such as the frequency of code contributions and the complexity of solved *issues*.

## **Epistemological Rigor and Methodological Architecture**

The LabTech report grounds its research design in the epistemological principles established by Ezequiel Ander-Egg. By defining science as a set of rational, certain, or probable knowledge obtained methodically and verified against reality, the study establishes a commitment to objectivity and systematic observation.34 This framework justifies the adoption of a mixed-methods approach, which triangulates quantitative data from GitHub repositories with qualitative insights from project diaries and student reports.15  
For the analysis of qualitative data, the study employs Laurence Bardin’s content analysis. This technique is described as a set of methodological instruments applied to diverse "discourses" to achieve an inference-based interpretation.36 The LabTech report follows Bardin's three-phase structure with technical precision:

1. **Pre-analysis:** This stage involves "floating reading" to internalize the data, followed by the selection of the corpus and the definition of indicators that will guide the final interpretation.37  
2. **Exploration of the Material:** This is the core analytical phase where the text is systematically coded and categorized into "meaning units" or "semantic drawers".38  
3. **Treatment of Results and Interpretation:** The final stage where the raw categorized data is transformed into meaningful insights, often using frequency analysis to identify dominant themes.38

The identification of categories such as "gain of technical autonomy" and "diversity of perspectives" highlights the effectiveness of this method in capturing the subjective experience of student growth.15 By using a "hermeneutics controlled" approach, the researchers ensure that their conclusions are rooted in the actual discourse of the participants rather than in their own biases.36

## **Analysis of Project Dynamics and Technical Output**

The technical activity within LabTech, as documented through GitHub repository metrics, provides a granular view of student engagement. The "activity heatmap" indicates a concentration of work during nocturnal hours and at the beginning of the week.15 This pattern is a direct reflection of the student profile at UDF, where many individuals are "working students" who balance academic and professional commitments. This "real-world" constraint adds a layer of authenticity to the ASF experience, as students must learn to manage their time and communicate asynchronously within their teams.  
The "burndown chart" and the "collaboration network" provide further evidence of the project's educational effectiveness. The burndown chart highlights the cyclical nature of software development, where periods of high activity (sprints) are followed by stabilization phases.15 The collaboration network shows that certain students act as central nodes or "mentors," facilitating the flow of information across the team.15 This self-organizing dynamic is a key objective of the SCRUM framework used by LabTech, as it promotes autonomy and peer-to-peer learning.  
However, the qualitative feedback also identifies areas for improvement. The "lack of organization and didactics" mentioned by some participants indicates that the transition from structured classroom learning to the autonomous environment of a Software Factory can be jarring for junior students.15 This finding suggests that while autonomy is a goal, it must be supported by a scaffolded mentorship structure that provides clearer guidance for those at the beginning of their programming journey.

## **Educational Implications and Strategic Recommendations**

The findings of the LabTech report have significant implications for the future of IT education. The success of the model in fostering collaborative value creation and high self-efficacy suggests that the ASF approach should be expanded and formalized across other computing programs. However, the identified limitation—the "ausência de um protocolo único de registro"—must be addressed to allow for more rigorous longitudinal analysis.15  
Strategic recommendations for the evolution of the LabTech initiative include:

1. **Standardization of Protocols:** Developing a unified guide for project diaries and meeting minutes to ensure data consistency across different teams.15  
2. **Scaffolded Mentorship:** Implementing a more structured onboarding process for junior students to mitigate the frustration associated with the transition to autonomous development.15  
3. **Real-World Stakeholder Integration:** Moving beyond simulated community needs toward direct partnerships with local NGOs or public agencies, providing students with the experience of managing real user requirements and feedback.14  
4. **Longitudinal Psychometric Tracking:** Administering the EntreComp and GSE instruments at the beginning and end of each student's tenure in the ASF to measure the "delta" of their competence development.

The LabTech ASF represents a sophisticated and successful integration of market needs, pedagogical theory, and psychological assessment. By bridging the gap between theory and practice, it prepares students not only to be technically proficient coders but also to be resilient, autonomous, and collaborative professionals capable of driving the digital transformation of Brasília and the broader Brazilian economy. The references and methodologies utilized in the report provide a robust and scientifically sound justification for the project’s continued existence and future expansion.

## **Detailed Psychometric Validation of the GSE Scale**

The General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSE), as utilized in the research, warrants a deeper examination of its internal structure to confirm the reliability of the scores obtained by the fourteen participants. According to the seminal factor analysis conducted by Sherer et al. (1982), the scale's items were meticulously selected based on their loadings to ensure they captured the core components of the construct: initiation, effort, and persistence.26  
In the original validation study involving 376 subjects, the General Self-efficacy subscale (Factor 1\) accounted for 26.5% of the total variance and yielded a Cronbach's alpha of 0.86, indicating high internal consistency.26 The LabTech report's use of a 20-item version follows a well-trodden path in psychological research where items are adapted to the specific cultural or professional context while maintaining the core psychometric properties of the original instrument.29  
The factor loadings for key items in the GSE provide a blueprint for understanding what is being measured in the LabTech students. For instance, item 1 ("When I make plans, I am certain I can make them work") has a loading of 0.486, while item 5 ("I give up on things before completing them" \- reverse coded) has a loading of 0.631.26 These loadings confirm that the scale is heavily weighted toward persistence in the face of adversity, a trait that is essential for the iterative nature of software development.

| Item Type (Sherer 1982\) | Example Descriptor | Factor Loading | Behavioral Alignment |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Initiation | "When I decide to do something, I go right to work on it." | 0.428 | Proactivity in Sprint starts. |
| Effort | "When I have something unpleasant to do, I stick to it until I finish." | 0.433 | Technical debt resolution. |
| Persistence | "If I can't do a job the first time, I keep trying until I can." | 0.560 | Debugging and refactoring. |
| Face of Adversity | "When unexpected problems occur, I don't handle them well." (R) | 0.547 | Incident management. |

The recoding of the six reverse items in the LabTech survey ($6-x$) is mathematically designed to align the direction of the scores with the construct of high self-efficacy. For example, a student who "Strongly Agrees" (5) with item 16 ("I give up easily") would have their score recoded to 1 ($6-5$), reflecting low self-efficacy. Conversely, a student who "Strongly Disagrees" (1) would have their score recoded to 5 ($6-1$), reflecting high self-efficacy.15 This meticulous approach to scoring ensures that the aggregate mean of $79.5$ is a valid representation of the group's perceived mastery.

The 15 competencies are distributed across the three areas mentioned previously, and the LabTech questionnaire focuses on the intersection of these competencies within the context of a software development project. For example, the "Resources" area includes "Self-awareness and self-efficacy" (2.1) and "Mobilizing others" (2.5), both of which are measured by the LabTech instrument.18

The high scores in "Criação de valor colaborativa" (3.79) align with the Expert level of the EntreComp progression model, where individuals are expected to "drive transformation and innovation" within their teams.47 This level of competence is typically characterized by high autonomy and the ability to generate value in complex, constantly changing environments—exactly the environment fostered by the LabTech ASF.18

## **Qualitative Themes in Student Discourse: A Bardin Analysis**

The processing of the open-ended responses using Bardin's content analysis provided three distinct "meaning units" that explain the quantitative success of the LabTech program. The first category, "Ganho de autonomia técnica," appeared in 66.7% of the valid responses, highlighting the project's success in fostering independent problem-solving.15 This is consistent with the "Taking the Initiative" competency of EntreComp and the "Persistence" dimension of the GSE scale.  
The second category, "Diversidade de perspectivas," underscores the multidisciplinary nature of the ASF. Participants noted that working with colleagues from different courses (e.g., Computer Science and Graphic Design) enriched the project and their own understanding of the software development lifecycle.15 This theme aligns with the "Working with Others" competency and reflects the social-efficacy component of Sherer's original framework.25  
The third category, "Sustentabilidade externa do programa," reflects a concern for the project's long-term impact and its ability to continue providing value to the community. This aligns with the "Ethical and sustainable thinking" competency (1.5) of EntreComp, which encourages learners to assess the long-term consequences of their ideas on society and the environment.18  
However, the critical feedback from participant R05 regarding "falta de organização e didática" points to a fourth, latent category: "Institutional Scaffolding".15 This suggests that while the "learning by doing" approach is effective for more advanced or naturally proactive students, those at the "Foundation" level of the EntreComp model require more explicit support and "building independence" (Intermediate level) before they can thrive in a fully autonomous factory setting.47

## **Conclusion and Methodological Integrity Check**

The exhaustive review of the LabTech experience report verifies that the references and methodologies cited are both existent and valid. The authors have effectively used the socioeconomic data from FecomercioSP 1, the regional economic insights from Agência Brasília 15, and the institutional goals from the UDF PPC 43 to build a compelling case for the project's relevance.  
The psychometric instruments—EntreComp and the General Self-Efficacy Scale—are applied with a high degree of adequacy, and the data analysis follows the established rigor of Ander-Egg's scientific definition and Bardin's content analysis.34 The results provide a clear narrative of a successful university extension project that effectively develops the technical and interpersonal competencies required by the modern IT industry. The identified limitations regarding the lack of standardized protocols provide a clear pathway for future research and program development, ensuring that LabTech can continue to serve as a beacon of applied computing education in Brasília.
